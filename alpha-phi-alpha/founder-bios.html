The Real Men

Seven Jewels, One First

In 1905, a small group of Black students at Cornell University started meeting to study together and look out for each other - far from home, at a school where they were often on their own. On December 4, 1906, that study group became Alpha Phi Alpha: the very first intercollegiate fraternity founded by African American men. The seven founders are honored as the Jewels, because each one was a treasure. These biographies are our kid-sized fan tribute to them.

1906

The Seven Jewels

&

Henry Arthur Callis

Jewel - The Doctor

One of the youngest students in the group, Henry became a medical doctor and spent his life healing people. He was also the last surviving Jewel, living into the 1970s - telling the Alpha story to new generations for nearly 70 years after founding day.

&

Charles Henry Chapman

Jewel - The Professor

Charles loved the land and the classroom. He became a professor of agriculture at Florida A&M University, teaching students how to grow things - crops, and themselves - for decades.

&

Eugene Kinckle Jones

Jewel - The Organizer

A giant for justice. Eugene led the National Urban League for about 30 years, fighting for jobs and fair housing for Black families, and advised leaders in Washington. Proof that one organized person can move a whole country.

&

George Biddle Kelley

Jewel - The First President

The fraternity's very first president. George was a civil engineer - a builder of bridges and roads - which fits, because he helped build the bridge that every Black fraternity and sorority after Alpha would cross.

&

Nathaniel Allison Murray

Jewel - The Teacher

Nathaniel spent his life as a teacher and later a farmer, staying connected to the fraternity he helped start for the rest of his days. A quiet Jewel whose steady love helped Alpha last.

&

Robert Harold Ogle

Jewel - The Lawyer

Sharp, steady and first-class. Robert became a lawyer and worked as a clerk in the United States Senate in Washington - a Black man serving at the heart of American government more than a century ago.

&

Vertner Woodson Tandy

Jewel - The Architect

Vertner became the first Black registered architect in New York State. He designed the fraternity's famous pin and real buildings you can still stand in front of today - including a mansion in Harlem built for Madam C.J. Walker, one of America's first self-made women millionaires.

A Fan Tribute

Why We Tell Their Stories

Seven students who were often left out built something that let everybody in. Every Black Greek-letter organization that came after - including this whole D9Legacy universe - walks through a door the Jewels opened.
