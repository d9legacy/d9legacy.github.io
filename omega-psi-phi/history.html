Since 1911

The History of Omega

Over one hundred years of friendship, learning and lifting as we climb.
1911

The Beginning

On the evening of November 17, 1911, inside the Science Building at Howard University in Washington, D.C., three students - Edgar Amos Love, Oscar James Cooper and Frank Coleman - sat down with their teacher, Professor Ernest Everett Just, and started a fraternity.

They picked a Greek phrase that means “friendship is essential to the soul,” and from its first letters came the name Omega Psi Phi. Their rules for life became the Cardinal Principles: Manhood, Scholarship, Perseverance and Uplift. Omega was the very first fraternity ever founded at a historically Black college.

1912 - 1930s

Growing Up

Omega spread fast. Beta chapter opened at Lincoln University in Pennsylvania, Gamma in Boston, and Lambda at the University of Southern California - the “pearl of the west coast.” Then Sigma chapter opened in Montreal, Canada, making Omega international.

In 1919 the fraternity published its first magazine, the Oracle. And after hearing the great historian Carter G. Woodson speak, Omega started Achievement Week to celebrate Black history and achievement - a tradition that still happens every November.

The Forties

Doctors, Songs and First Homes

Brother Dr. Charles Drew perfected how to store blood plasma - a discovery that has saved millions of lives around the world. In 1949 the fraternity bought its first national headquarters building in Washington, D.C., and named its scholarship fund after Dr. Drew.

The Fifties

Standing Up for What’s Right

Omega men went to work for fairness. Lawyers like Spottswood Robinson, Oliver Hill and James Nabrit fought the court battles that ended segregated schools, leading to the famous Brown v. Board of Education decision. Chapters raised tens of thousands of dollars for the NAACP, and Omega opened a chapter in Liberia, Africa.

The Sixties

The March and the Dream

In 1963, Brother Bayard Rustin helped organize the March on Washington, where Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. gave his “I Have a Dream” speech. In 1966, Brother Robert C. Weaver became the first Black American to serve in a president’s cabinet. Omega turned 50 years old, and all three living founders came to the party.

The Seventies

Remembering the Founders

Omega said goodbye to its founders - Cooper in 1972 and Love, the last one, in 1974. In 1975 a granite monument to all four founders was dedicated at Howard University, right near where the fraternity was born. In 1979, Omega promised $250,000 to the United Negro College Fund to help students pay for college.

The Eighties & Nineties

New Heights

In 1986 the world lost Brother Ronald McNair, an astronaut who died aboard the Space Shuttle Challenger - a hero who reached for the stars. In 1990, Brother L. Douglas Wilder became the first elected Black governor in American history. Omega moved its headquarters to Decatur, Georgia, and held its first Youth Leadership Conference to teach young men about character and manners.

Today

All Omega, All the Time

In 2011, more than 10,000 Omega men came back to Washington, D.C. to celebrate the fraternity’s 100th birthday. Today there are more than 700 chapters all over the world - in the United States, Canada, the Bahamas, Ghana, Germany, Japan, the United Kingdom and beyond.

And in 2026, the D9Legacy chapter opened right here - because friendship is still essential to the soul, and we are still lifting as we climb.
